\section*{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================
This report tests reinforcement-learning algorithms that decide when to measure a qubit, so that its
frequency is learned as precisely as physically possible. The work implements adaptive Bayesian
experimental design for single-qubit Ramsey frequency estimation. A sequential Monte Carlo filter
maintains a posterior over the unknown frequency $\omega$, and a policy reads that posterior
and picks the interrogation time $t$ for the next shot. The measurement outcome then updates the posterior with the particle filter, and the cycle repeats. The policy is trained either by reinforcement learning (TRPO) or
by a gradient-free evolutionary search (CEM), following Fiderer, Schuff \& Braun,
Neural-network heuristics for adaptive Bayesian quantum estimation.
Two learned policies are built on the same Gymnasium environment, and are
evaluated on \num{10000} randomly selected $\omega$.
TRPO and CEM agree to within 1.7\% ($2.173\times10^{-3}$ vs $2.137\times10^{-3}$ Bayes risk),
consistent with the results reported in the paper.

\section{The experiment}
\label{sec:the-experiment}
% ===========================================================================
The system is a single qubit undergoing Ramsey interferometry in the presence of pure dephasing:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Prepare $|+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$.
  \item Let it precess freely under $H = \tfrac{\omega}{2}\sigma_z$ for a time $t$. This is the
  control variable - the one thing the experimenter chooses.
  \item Apply a $\pi/2$ read-out pulse and measure in the computational basis.
  \item Record the binary outcome $d \in \{0, 1\}$.
\end{enumerate}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The likelihood}
\label{sec:the-likelihood}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Solving the Lindblad pure-dephasing master equation and applying the Born rule gives the outcome
probability as in  %TODO

\begin{align}
P(d=0 \mid \omega, t)
  &= e^{-t/T_2}\cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right) + \frac{1 - e^{-t/T_2}}{2} \nonumber \\
  &\equiv \frac{1}{2}\bigl[\, 1 + e^{-t/T_2}\cos(\omega t) \,\bigr],
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{align}
and $P(d=1) = 1 - P(d=0)$. 
\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{The likelihood.}
  \label{tab:the-likelihood}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  term & meaning \\
  \midrule
  $\cos^2(\omega t/2)$ & this is what carries information about $\omega$ \\
  $v(t) = e^{-t/T_2}$ & the visibility, or coherent fraction \\
  $(1-v)/2$ & the fully dephased fraction, which returns a coin flip and tells you nothing \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}
As $t \to \infty$ the visibility vanishes, $P(d=0) \to 1/2$, and the measurement becomes pure noise.
Decoherence therefore imposes a hard ceiling on how long it is worth interrogating.




% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Why long interrogation times stop helping}
\label{sec:aside-why-long-interrogation-times-stop-helping}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Fisher information is the right tool for quantifying how much one shot can say about $\omega$.
Differentiating the likelihood gives the single-shot (classical) Fisher information

\begin{equation}
I(\omega; t) = \frac{\left(\partial_\omega P\right)^2}{P\,(1-P)}
  = \frac{t^2\,v^2\sin^2(\omega t)}{1 - v^2\cos^2(\omega t)},
\qquad v = e^{-t/T_2}.
\label{eq:fisher-info}
\end{equation}

Without decoherence ($v \to 1$) this reduces to $I = t^2$: information grows quadratically in the
interrogation time and is independent of $\omega$. With finite $T_2$ the factor $v^2 =
e^{-2t/T_2}$ supplies the envelope $t^2 e^{-2t/T_2}$, which peaks at $t = T_2$. The corresponding
bound is $I_{\max} \approx e^{-2}T_2^2 = 0.135\,T_2^2$; for $T_2 = 10$ the actual single-shot
optimum sits at $t^\star \approx 8.4$-$9.5$, weakly $\omega$-dependent. For $t \gg T_2$ the
envelope decays exponentially and $I \to 0$: a fully dephased measurement returns a fair coin and
carries no information. This is the paper's observation that ``times which exceed $T_2$ tend to
yield no information, which explains why the Bayes risk saturates''.

Cram\'er--Rao therefore bounds any fixed schedule: with $N$ shots,
$\mathrm{Var}(\omega) \ge 1/\sum_k I(\omega; t_k)$. Since per-shot information is bounded above,
the only way to keep improving is to hold the per-shot information near its $t \approx T_2$
ceiling - which a policy may do only once the posterior has narrowed.
 This is the sanity check Fisher information provides - it is not the objective.


% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The competing constraint: phase ambiguity}
\label{sec:the-competing-constraint-phase-ambiguity}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Fisher information is a local quantity, and maximising it is the wrong move while the prior
is still broad.
A good policy must therefore trade off:

\begin{itemize}
  \item small $t$ - unambiguous, but low information per shot ($I \sim t^2$).
  \item $t \approx T_2$ - maximum information per shot, but broad prior.
  \item $t \gg T_2$ - decohered, zero information, strictly wasted.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The figure of merit: Bayes risk}
\label{sec:the-figure-of-merit-bayes-risk}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is a Bayesian estimation problem, and the quantity everything is judged by is the Bayes risk -
Fiderer Eq. (1):

\begin{equation}
r\bigl(h \mid p(\theta)\bigr) = \mathbb{E}_{D_k}\bigl[\, \mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\theta \mid D_k)
\,\bigr],
\label{eq:bayes-risk}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the experiment-design heuristic, $D_k = (d_1,\dots,d_k)$ the measurement record, and
smaller is better. It is the risk of the quadratic loss $L(\hat\theta_k, \theta) =
\lVert\hat\theta_k - \theta\rVert_2^2$ under the Bayes estimator $\hat\theta_k(D_k) =
\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D_k]$. That is, the posterior mean - which is exactly what this
implementation reports.

With the Bayes estimator $\hat\theta_k(D_k) = \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid D_k]$,

\begin{equation}
r = \mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{D_k\mid\theta}\!\left[\lVert \hat\theta_k -
\theta \rVert_2^2\right]\right]
  = \mathbb{E}_{D_k}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{\theta\mid D_k}\!\left[\lVert \hat\theta_k -
\theta \rVert_2^2\right]\right]
  = \mathbb{E}_{D_k}\!\left[\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\theta \mid D_k)\right],
\end{equation}

using Bayes' law to swap the conditioning and the variance-minimising property of the posterior
mean. For a single parameter the trace is the scalar posterior variance.

Here the trace is over a $1\times1$ covariance, and the expectation is averaged over true $\omega$
values. So the Bayes risk is precisely the mean final posterior variance.

The paper is explicit that the frequentist route goes ``with the Cram\'er-Rao bound formalism
by maximizing the quantum Fisher information''. It
deliberately takes the Bayesian route instead. Fisher information is a useful sanity check here
 but it is not the objective.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The reward}
\label{sec:the-reward}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
The reward is the reduction in traced posterior covariance at each step - Fiderer Eq. (2):

\begin{align}
R(D_k) &= \mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\theta \mid D_{k-1}) - \mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\theta \mid
D_k) \nonumber \\
       &= V_{k-1} - V_k,
\label{eq:reward}
\end{align}
with $V_k = \sum_i w_k^i\,(\xi^i - \bar{\xi})^2$. This is not an arbitrary choice, and it is not a
weaker substitute for an information-gain reward. The paper's reasoning: the negative Bayes risk is
the obvious reward but is too slow to compute inside a training loop. Eq. (2) is cheap in the
SMC framework and telescopes to it. Eq. (3), for $N$ experiments per episode:

\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{D_N}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N} R(D_j)\right] = \mathrm{const} - r\bigl(h \mid p(\theta)\bigr),
\qquad \mathrm{const} = \mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\theta).
\label{eq:telescope}
\end{equation}
So maximising the episode return minimises the Bayes risk. The paper additionally sets $\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(\theta)$ to
its exact prior value ($1/12$ for $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$) as a numerical constant rather than
recomputing it from a finite particle sample, so the reward carries no particle-filter noise from
the first step.

\subsection{SMC with Liu--West}
\label{sec:smc-liu-west}

We estimate a single fixed parameter $\omega$, chosen once at the start of every run and never
evolving. Ordinary particle filters rely on the parameter randomly moving at each step to
keep the particles spread out. Since $\omega$ is frozen, nothing spreads them so resampling collapses every particle onto a few
duplicated values.
Liu--West resampling fixes this by adding random noise to
each particle while preserving the posterior's mean and variance.

\paragraph{Particle system.}
We carry $N$ parameter particles $\{\xi^i\}$ with log-weights $\{\log w_k^i\}$.
Shapes are \texttt{(N, 1)} / \texttt{(N,)} in the NumPy convention.

\paragraph{Bayes update.}
Each new observation $y_k$ at step $k$ reweights particles:
\begin{equation}
\log w_k^i \leftarrow \log w_k^i
  + \log p(y_k \mid \xi^i)
  - \operatorname{logsumexp}(\cdot).
\label{eq:bayes-update}
\end{equation}
Renormalised every step, so the weights always sum to one, and only the
weights change: $\omega$ is a static parameter, so its particle
locations are untouched by the update.

\paragraph{Effective sample size.}
The effective sample size $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$,
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \Bigl(\textstyle\sum_i (w_k^i)^{2}\Bigr)^{-1} \in [1, N],
\end{equation}
measures how concentrated the weights have become. Resampling fires when
$N_{\mathrm{eff}} < 0.75N$ - a deliberately aggressive threshold, which performed best in testing.

\paragraph{Liu--West kernel resampling $(a = 0.98)$.}
When the threshold is crossed, each particle is shrunk toward the weighted mean
$\bar{\xi}$ and jittered with noise:
\begin{equation}
\xi^i \leftarrow a\,\hat{\xi}^i + (1-a)\,\bar{\xi} + \varepsilon^i,
\qquad \varepsilon^i \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\; h^{2}\Sigma\bigr),
\qquad h^{2} = 1 - a^{2},
\label{eq:liu-west}
\end{equation}
where $\Sigma$ is the covariance of the particles, weighted by $w_k^i$.

The shrinkage toward the weighted mean exactly cancels the variance added by the
jitter,
\begin{equation}
a^{2}\Sigma + h^{2}\Sigma
  = \bigl(a^{2} + (1-a^{2})\bigr)\Sigma = \Sigma,
\end{equation}
so the posterior's mean and variance are preserved. The particles' locations move during
resampling, so weakly supported particles are removed while strongly supported ones are duplicated.

The CEM network squashes its output structurally, $t = t_{\min} + (t_{\max}-t_{\min})\,\sigma(z)$,
so it can never emit an out-of-range time. The TRPO actor is an unbounded Gaussian whose sample is
clipped in the environment step.

\subsubsection{1. TRPO}
\label{sec:method-trpo}
The policy reads a 32-dimensional continuous observation, consisting of the posterior mean, the
posterior variance and the last 30 interrogation times, and emits an unbounded Gaussian sample of
$t$, which is clipped to $[0.1, 3000]$ in the environment step. Both the policy and the value head
are multilayer perceptrons with two hidden layers of 256 units. The optimiser is the trust-region
policy optimiser from \texttt{sb3-contrib}, with a target KL of $10^{-2}$ and $\gamma = 0.99$. TRPO
is trained from scratch for $10^7$ timesteps.

Training is the standard trust-region loop, one update per rollout batch:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Collect experience. The agent plays episodes in the adaptive SMC environment. Each episode
  draws one true frequency, runs \num{100} measurements, observes the posterior mean, the posterior
  variance and the last 30 interrogation times, and receives the reward $V_{k-1} - V_k$. \num{2048}
  environment steps are gathered per update.
  \item Estimate advantages. Generalised advantage estimation with $\gamma = 0.99$ and $\lambda =
  0.95$ converts the reward sequence into a normalised advantage $\hat A$ for every step, using the
  value head as the baseline.
  \item Fit the value function. The critic takes \num{10} gradient steps per update, which lowers
  the variance of $\hat A$.
  \item Take a trust-region step. The actor maximises the
  $\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a\mid s)}\,\hat
  A\right]$ subject to $\mathbb{E}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}} \,\|\,
  \pi_\theta)\right] \le 10^{-2}$. The search direction is the natural gradient, obtained by
  conjugate gradients (at most \num{15} iterations, damping $0.1$). A backtracking line search then
  shrinks the step by $0.8$ until the KL constraint holds and the surrogate improves. If no such
  step exists, the update is reverted.
  \item Repeat. Steps 1-4 run for $10^5$ episodes ($10^7$ timesteps). The policy used for
  evaluation is the network after the last update.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{2. CEM}
\label{sec:method-cem}
A gradient-free cross-entropy method over the flat 545-dimensional weight vector of a
single-hidden-layer time policy with 16 units, a tanh activation and a sigmoid-squashed output.
Population 1000, elite fraction 0.1, initial standard deviation 1.0, with 100-step episodes and 2000
particles.

Training is a generation loop over that weight vector:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Initialise the search distribution. The network's parameters are flattened into a
  \num{545}-dimensional vector $\theta$, and the search starts from $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1.0$.
  \item Sample a population. Draw \num{1000} candidates $\theta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.
  \item Score each candidate. Each $\theta_i$ is copied into the network, which maps the observation
  through \num{16} tanh units and squashes its output with a sigmoid, $t = t_{\min} + (t_{\max} -
  t_{\min})\,\sigma(z)$. The candidate's return is the total reward of one full episode played at
  that generation's true frequency.
  \item Select the elite. Rank the \num{1000} candidates by return and keep the best \num{100}
  (elite fraction $0.1$).
  \item Refit the distribution. Set $\mu$ to the elite mean and $\sigma$ to the elite standard
  deviation, so the next generation samples around the better parameters.
  \item Repeat. Steps 2-5 run for \num{100} generations. The returned policy is the final $\mu$,
  not a sampled population member.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Training}
\label{sec:training}
% ===========================================================================
TRPO. Training runs for $10^5$ episodes of 100 steps, or $10^7$ timesteps, with \num{2000}
particles, policy and value networks of two hidden layers of 256 units each, and a target KL of
$10^{-2}$. Checkpoints are written every \num{50000} timesteps. A partially trained policy can then
be evaluated at any point, and an interrupted run costs at most one interval.

TRPO is trained from scratch.

CEM. Training runs for 100 generations at population 1000 and elite fraction 0.1, with 2000
particles and 100-step episodes, optimising the flat 545-dimensional weight vector of the
single-hidden-layer time policy of 16 units. This takes about 54 minutes on CPU, roughly 32\,s per
generation.

Logging. Logged per episode (TRPO) or per generation (CEM): mean reward, mean initial variance, mean
final variance, mean effective sample size, mean interrogation time and true frequency. Plus maximum
reward, mean sigma and the norm of $\mu$ for CEM. Parameters capture the full hyperparameter set.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
% ===========================================================================
Both policies are scored through one shared NumPy reference harness, which runs at float64 and
follows one true frequency at a time. The harness is method-agnostic. The SMC filter, the
measurement model and the scoring code are identical for TRPO and CEM. The only method-specific part
is the function that maps the posterior summary to the next interrogation time $t$. The procedure
below is therefore the evaluation of both policies.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Draw the test frequencies. Draw $N$ true frequencies $\omega \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$
  from a fixed seed. The draw is independent of the training draw.
  \item Initialise the filter. Represent the prior by \num{2000} particles drawn
  $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ with uniform weights. This is the belief before the first measurement.
  \item Run one episode. Repeat the following \num{125} times, once per measurement:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Read the belief. Normalise the particle weights, compute the posterior mean and variance
    from them. Assemble the observation the policy sees: the mean, the variance, then the last
    \num{30} chosen interrogation times (all zero before the first step).
    \item Choose the interrogation time. The policy maps that observation to a single $t$, clipped
    to $[0.1, 3000]$.
    \item Simulate the measurement. Draw the binary outcome $d \in \{0,1\}$ from $\Pr(d = 0 \mid
    \omega, t) = e^{-t/T_2}\cos^2(\omega t/2) + (1 - e^{-t/T_2})/2$ with $T_2 = 10$, evaluated at
    the true $\omega$ of the episode.
    \item Update the posterior. Reweight every particle by the likelihood of the observed outcome
    $d$ at its own $\omega$.
    \item Resample if needed. If the effective sample size falls below $0.75 \times \num{2000} =
    \num{1500}$, resample the particles with the Liu-West scheme to restore weight diversity before
    the next step.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Score the episode. Record that episode's final posterior variance - one number per
  frequency.
  \item Average. Average the $N$ final variances. For this single-parameter, experiment-limited
  problem the traced posterior covariance averaged over true $\omega$ is exactly the final posterior
  variance, so this average is the Bayes risk of
  Eq. (1). Lower is better.
\end{enumerate}

The validation reported below uses $N = \num{10000}$ with number of frequencies defaulting to \num{2000}.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:summary}

This report tested reinforcement-learning algorithms that decide when to measure a qubit, so that
its frequency is learned as precisely as physically possible. The work implemented adaptive
Bayesian experimental design for single-qubit Ramsey frequency estimation. A sequential Monte
Carlo filter maintains the posterior over $\omega$, and the trained policy maps that posterior
summary to the interrogation time $t$ for the next shot. The next measurement outcome corrects
the posterior, and the cycle repeats. The policy was trained either by reinforcement learning
(TRPO) or by a gradient-free evolutionary search (CEM), following Fiderer, Schuff \& Braun,
Neural-network heuristics for adaptive Bayesian quantum estimation. Two learned policies
were built on the same Gymnasium environment and evaluated on \num{10000} randomly selected
$\omega$. TRPO and CEM reach similar performance ($2.173\times10^{-3}$ vs $2.137\times10^{-3}$
Bayes risk). The two policies reach this tie by different strategies: the TRPO
policy chooses times that are generally shorter than those chosen by the CEM policy, settling
around $t \approx 4$ while CEM settles around $t \approx 6.7$. This tie is consistent with the
paper, which reports TRPO outperforming CEM only when there is limited compute resource available.
